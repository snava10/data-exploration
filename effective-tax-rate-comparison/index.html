What share of a salary actually goes in tax?

Published

One number per place — personal income tax plus mandatory employee payroll contributions, divided by gross salary, for a single employee with no children — computed two fair ways: at the same US-dollar salary converted at market exchange rates, and at each country’s own average wage. The two methods answer different questions, and the ranking changes between them.

Disclaimer: This analysis represents a broad overview of tax systems in major developed countries. The data may contain minor errors and should not be considered a comprehensive or definitive analysis.

Two ways to ask the question

Method A

The same salary in US dollars

Take US$50,000, US$100,000 and US$150,000, convert each into local currency at market exchange rates, and run the local tax model on that salary. This answers the relocation question: “I have a US$100,000-equivalent offer — what would I actually pay if I moved?” It is transparent and intuitive, but market exchange rates swing, and they say nothing about local wage levels or prices — US$100,000 is a top-decile salary in some of these places and a middling one in others.

Method B

Each country’s own average wage

Anchor the taxpayer to each country’s own average wage instead — the approach the OECD’s Taxing Wages uses. This answers the systems question: “How does each tax system treat its own typical worker?” The rate itself is unit-free, so no currency conversion is needed to compare rates; the wage amounts are also shown in PPP (purchasing-power-parity) dollars, which convert at rates that equalise purchasing power rather than at market exchange rates.

Neither method is “the truth” on its own — they measure different things. Reading them together shows which places tax a given dollar salary heavily, which tax their own typical worker heavily, and where those two answers diverge.

Method A — the same salary in US dollars (market exchange rates)

The three US-dollar benchmarks are converted with the Federal Reserve’s official G.5 September 2026 monthly-average rates (per US$1: EUR 0.869, GBP 0.746, CHF 0.820, AUD 1.405, CAD 1.397, JPY 156.4, SGD 1.273, HKD 7.843, NOK 9.391, DKK 6.498, TWD 31.738) and rounded to the nearest 100 in local currency (nearest 10,000 for yen). At the US$100,000 benchmark, for example, the local salary is £74,600 in the UK, €86,900 in the euro-area rows, ¥15,640,000 in Japan and NT$3,173,800 in Taiwan.

At US$50,000

US$50,000 converted at market rates is a moderate professional salary in most of these places but a high one in some — NT$1,586,900 in Taiwan is roughly double the average wage there. Taiwan (8.4%) and Hong Kong (10.5%) are the lowest; the no-state-tax US rows (Texas, Florida) come next at 15.3%. Italy (34.9%) and Germany (33.8%) are already the heaviest at this level, mostly through employee social contributions levied from the first euro.

Watch Singapore at this level. At S$63,600 income tax is only 3.5%, yet the combined rate is 23.5% — because the employee CPF contribution is the full 20% of salary, with the CPF ceiling not yet in the way. The CPF share is compulsory savings in the employee’s own account, not general tax revenue — so on a tax-only view Singapore would sit near the bottom of this table instead.

At US$100,000 the ranking reshuffles. Method B — the typical worker: the average wage (OECD-style)

Here the salary is each jurisdiction’s own average annual wage, so every row describes the typical worker in that system rather than a transplanted dollar salary. For OECD members the wage is the OECD Taxing Wages 2026 Secretariat estimate for 2025; all five US rows use the US national average wage of US$73,520. Where the OECD does not publish an average wage, national statistics are used: Singapore’s Ministry of Manpower average monthly earnings (≈S$6,300 a month, S$76,000 a year used here), Hong Kong’s Census and Statistics Department median monthly wage (HK$21,200 in 2025 — a median, not a mean), and Taiwan’s DGBAS average annual total earnings (NT$806,448 in 2025, including bonuses and other irregular earnings). The PPP-dollar column converts each wage at purchasing-power-parity rates, so the amounts are comparable in what they buy: Switzerland’s average wage (PPP $100,715) is the highest in the table, Estonia’s (PPP $42,333) and Hong Kong’s (PPP $43,487) the lowest.

Taiwan (5.5%) and Hong Kong (7.0%) are again the lowest — at these wage levels their income taxes are minimal and contributions are capped or small. Germany (38.7%) is the highest for the typical worker, ahead of Denmark (34.7%) and Italy (32.6%): Germany’s employee contributions (21.5% of gross) barely fall at its high average wage of €66,700. France’s typical worker pays 30.7%, of which only 7.6 points are income tax — the rest is employee contributions.

Singapore at the average wage. At S$76,000 the employee CPF contribution is still the full 20% (the S$102,000 ceiling does not bind), so the combined rate is 24.0% even though income tax is only 4.0% — and it is slightly higher than Singapore’s rate at the US$100,000 benchmark in Method A (23.1%), where the ceiling already caps the contribution. As ever, the CPF share is compulsory savings in the employee’s own account.

How to read this

The figure shown is the employee’s overall effective tax rate: income tax plus mandatory employee social security contributions, expressed as a percentage of gross salary. That is the standard payslip view: it is money you do not receive. Where part of it is really your own savings — Singapore CPF, Hong Kong’s MPF, Estonia’s funded pension — the tables flag it.

What is left out: VAT / sales tax and other consumption taxes, property taxes, and employer-side contributions. Employer contributions are large in several European systems. For context, OECD Taxing Wages 2026 puts the full tax wedge including employer costs at the average wage at 52.5% in Belgium, 49.3% in Germany and 47.2% in France — a different, broader measure than the employee-payslip rate used here.

The two methods measure different things. Method A’s rates move with the exchange rate — a weaker pound makes the UK’s converted salary (and, through the bracket structure, its rate) fall without any UK policy change. Method B’s rates depend on the wage anchor: averages are means pulled up by top earners, Hong Kong’s anchor is a median, and Taiwan’s includes bonuses — so small cross-country differences in Method B should not be over-read.

Place matters inside countries too: US states, Swiss cantons, Canadian provinces (Ontario used here), Spanish autonomous communities (Madrid used here) and Italian regional surtaxes all move the number. New York City adds roughly 3.5 points on top of New York State.

Method in brief

Tax models. For each jurisdiction: apply the official 2026 (or latest 2025) income-tax schedule, standard deductions/allowances and credits available to a single childless employee, plus mandatory employee social contributions with their official caps and ceilings. Divide the total by gross salary. Figures cross-checked for ordering against OECD Taxing Wages 2026 (2025 data).

Method A conversions use the Federal Reserve G.5 September 2026 monthly-average rates (currency units per US$1): EUR 0.869, GBP 0.746, CHF 0.820, AUD 1.405, CAD 1.397, JPY 156.4, SGD 1.273, HKD 7.843, NOK 9.391, DKK 6.498, TWD 31.738. Local salaries are rounded to the nearest 100 (yen to the nearest 10,000) before the tax model is applied.

Method B wages and PPPs. Method B follows the OECD Taxing Wages convention: the taxpayer is anchored to each country’s own average wage, and dollar equivalents use purchasing-power-parity (PPP) conversion, which equalises purchasing power across countries, rather than market exchange rates. Method A is the market-exchange-rate alternative: it converts a common US-dollar salary at Federal Reserve G.5 market rates. Average wages: OECD Taxing Wages 2026 Secretariat estimates (2025) for OECD members; US national average wage US$73,520 for all US rows; Singapore MOM average monthly earnings ≈S$6,300 (S$76,000 a year used); Hong Kong C&SD median monthly wage HK$21,200 (2025); Taiwan DGBAS average annual total earnings NT$806,448 (2025). PPP conversion factors (local currency units per international dollar, OECD 2024 / IMF estimates): GBP 0.70; EUR 0.70 (Germany, France), 0.60 (Spain, Italy, Estonia), 0.80 (Netherlands, Ireland); CHF 1.00; AUD 1.40; JPY 94.9; CAD 1.20; NOK 8.9; DKK 6.1; SGD 0.85; HKD 5.85; TWD 14.6; USD 1.0.

  • UK at £111,900 (US$150,000 benchmark): the personal allowance is being withdrawn through the £100,000–£125,140 taper; at £74,600 (US$100,000) and at the average wage (£51,310) it is fully intact.
  • Singapore: the employee CPF contribution is 20% of salary up to the S$102,000 annual ceiling — the full 20.0% at S$63,600 and at the average wage (S$76,000), then 16.0% at S$127,300 and 10.7% at S$190,900 once the ceiling binds.
  • Hong Kong: salaries tax plus the Mandatory Provident Fund (MPF) employee contribution — 5% capped at HK$18,000/year (4.6% of HK$392,200; 2.3% of HK$784,300; 1.5% of HK$1,176,500; the full 5.0% at the HK$254,400 wage anchor, where the cap does not bind). MPF is the individual’s own retirement account, like Singapore’s CPF.
  • Taiwan: capped insured salaries keep employee labour + health insurance at 1.8–3.3% of gross across all rows; the rate differences are driven by income tax.
  • Germany excludes church tax; employee social contributions are capped, falling from 21.6% of gross at €43,500 to 13.4% at €130,400 (21.5% at the €66,700 average wage).
  • Netherlands: social premiums are levied inside the income-tax brackets and sit in the income-tax column throughout; labour and general tax credits are fully phased out at €130,400; occupational pension contributions (employer-specific) excluded.
  • Denmark: the 8% AM-bidrag (labour-market contribution) is counted in the employee-contributions column here; the OECD counts it as income tax, so Denmark’s split differs from Taxing Wages even where totals are close. Denmark’s figures use 2025 rules; a Danish tax reform takes effect from 2026.
  • Switzerland (Zurich) excludes mandatory private health-insurance premiums, roughly 3% of salary.
  • Australia includes the 2% Medicare levy in the income-tax column; there are no separate employee social contributions.
  • Italy (Rome): IRPEF (23% / 33% / 43% for 2026) plus the Lazio regional surtax and the Rome municipal surtax (0.9%); employee INPS contributions are subject to the official annual ceiling.
  • Canada (Ontario): federal + Ontario income tax plus Canada Pension Plan (including CPP2) and Employment Insurance. Ontario is the most populous province; other provinces differ.
  • Norway: 22% ordinary income tax + bracket tax (trinnskatt) + 7.8% employee national insurance.
