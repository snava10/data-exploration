Data exploration · UK water companies

One address, one water company

In England and Wales you cannot choose who supplies your water. Sixteen regulated companies hold exclusive territories — regional monopolies set by law, not by competition — and Scotland and Northern Ireland each have a single national provider. These maps colour every area by its monopoly supplier, drawn from Ofwat's official boundary data.

The catch: tap water and sewerage are separate monopolies. In the areas run by water-only companies, a different company takes the wastewater away — so there are two maps. Both are shown in full below: every territory, every company, at a glance.

So, can you switch? As a household, no — anywhere in the UK. The one exception in the system is for businesses, and even that choice is narrower than it sounds, as the section below the maps shows.

So you can find your bearings, both maps also carry dotted lines for the ceremonial counties — the familiar named areas people actually use: Suffolk, Norfolk, Cambridgeshire, Oxfordshire, Kent, Devon, Northumberland and the rest, with London and the other big-city counties drawn as single areas rather than a thicket of boroughs and districts. Scotland is divided into its lieutenancy areas, Wales into its preserved counties and Northern Ireland into its six counties — 73 areas in all, in place of the 218 council areas a finer layer would draw. Dark dots mark the main cities — London, Edinburgh, Cardiff, Belfast and the big English cities — each named where it sits, so you can place a territory against places you know. To look closer at crowded areas such as London and the South East, use the + / − buttons on each map (or scroll, pinch and drag) — Reset returns to the full view. The company and city names shrink as you zoom in, so the lettering never swamps the area you are inspecting — but only down to a floor, so they stay readable however far in you go. Can you switch? Households are stuck; businesses get a partial choice

The maps above are not a snapshot of a market — they are the whole market. For a household there is no switching service, no comparison site and no rival offer to weigh up: the company coloured over your address is the only company legally appointed to supply you, and it stays your supplier until you move house. The choice that does exist is for non-household customers, and it stops at the billing layer.

Households · No choice

Stuck with whoever serves your street

In every part of the UK, a home gets its water and sewerage from the one company appointed for its area. There is no household retail market to open: when the English business market was created, households were explicitly left out of it, and the same is true in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

Unhappy with your company? You cannot leave it. Your protections are regulation, not competition — Ofwat caps what companies can charge through five-yearly price reviews, and the Consumer Council for Water handles complaints. Your bill, your leakage record and your sewage spills all belong to a company you never chose.

Businesses · Partial choice

You can choose who bills you — not who supplies you

Since April 2017, businesses, charities and public-sector bodies in England can choose their water retailer; Scotland opened the same choice in 2008, the first market of its kind in the world. A retailer reads the meter, sends the bill and handles customer service, and competing retailers can differ on price and service.

But the wholesale layer never changes hands: the water still arrives through the same pipes, from the same regional monopoly shown on these maps, and the same company still takes the sewage away. In Wales, only very large users — over 50 million litres a year — can switch retailer; everywhere else, and throughout Northern Ireland, businesses are as fixed as households.

The one crack in the household picture is at the edges: on some new housing developments an Ofwat-appointed “new appointment” (NAV) company, rather than the regional incumbent, provides the water and sewerage — but the developer chooses it, not the people who move in. For everyone else, the answer to “can I change my water company?” is simply no: you are a customer of a monopoly by address, not by choice.

History: why the system is the way it is

None of this was designed as a market. The territories on the maps are the product of two decisions — one about geography in the 1970s, one about ownership in the 1980s — laid on top of a much older patchwork. Water is also a natural monopoly: one network of pipes and sewers can serve an area far more cheaply than two rival networks dug under the same streets, so the law appoints a single company for each area and uses regulation, not rivalry, to keep it in check.

  1. 1800s–1973 A Victorian patchwork Water arrived through a mixture of private companies and town councils, and sewerage through yet more local bodies — well over a thousand separate undertakings in all. From the late nineteenth century councils took over many private suppliers, but a group of private water-only companies survived, supplying about a quarter of the population in England and Wales.
  2. 1974 Ten authorities, drawn around rivers — not counties The Water Act 1973 swept the local bodies in England and Wales into ten public regional water authorities, each covering a river basin so that supply, sewage treatment and river management could be planned for a whole catchment. That is why the boundaries on these maps follow watersheds and ignore county lines. The private water-only companies were left untouched — the origin of the split between the two maps, with one company on the taps and another on the drains.
  3. 1989 Privatisation — in England and Wales only The Water Act 1989 turned the ten authorities into ten private water-and-sewerage companies and floated them, while river and environmental regulation passed to a new public authority. Because customers could not choose their supplier, the same reforms created Ofwat to act in place of competition, capping prices in five-yearly reviews. The water-only companies were brought under the same regulator but kept their territories.
  4. 1989–2007 Scotland and Northern Ireland take a different path Privatisation never happened there. Scotland’s services stayed public — after public opposition in the 1990s, its three regional authorities were merged in 2002 into the publicly owned Scottish Water. In Northern Ireland, water stayed with government until the government-owned NI Water was created in 2007. Each nation therefore has the single national provider shown on the maps.
  5. Since 1989 Consolidation, and one Welsh exception Mergers shrank the 29 water-only companies that existed at privatisation into the handful of larger groupings listed below, and a boundary realignment in Wales in 2018 created Hafren Dyfrdwy as an eleventh water-and-sewerage company. In 2001, Dŵr Cymru Welsh Water became not-for-profit under Glas Cymru — the one large company in the system with no shareholders. The underlying pattern, though, is unchanged since 1989.

So the present map is not the outcome of companies winning customers. It is the 1974 river-basin map, sold as it stood in 1989, lightly reshuffled since — and kept a monopoly on purpose, because duplicating the pipes was judged worse than regulating the company that owns them.

Is Thames Water really the only supplier in London?

Mostly — but not entirely. Thames Water is the water-and-sewerage company for most of Greater London, and it handles the sewerage for almost all of it. But in the north-west boroughs (Harrow, Hillingdon, Barnet, Brent, Ealing, Enfield) and out into Hertfordshire, the tap water comes from Affinity Water; in the south, around Sutton and Croydon, it comes from SES Water; and on the north-east fringe, Essex & Suffolk Water (part of Northumbrian Water) supplies parts of Havering and neighbouring areas. Look at the sewerage map and London turns a single colour: Thames's wastewater population is larger than its water population precisely because it treats the sewage for those other companies' customers.

What would fix rising bills? Three approaches, side by side

Bills in England and Wales are rising 36% on average by 2030 to pay for £104bn of investment after decades of underinvestment. Two ideas dominate the debate — competition and nationalisation — plus a third: keep the companies private but regulate them much more tightly. Here they are, side by side.

1. More competition

Break the monopolies and let rivals fight on price.

How it would work
Not by duplicating pipes — nobody proposes that. Instead: let households choose who bills them, let rival firms run new developments, and tender big projects and river clean-ups to the lowest bidder.
For
Rivals already connect about a fifth of new homes, and tendering delivered the Tideway tunnel cheaply. Pressure, where it can exist, works.
Against
Ofwat put the best case for household switching at only about £8 per customer per year. The pipes stay a monopoly either way.
Effect on bills
Small. Helpful around the edges, not a cure for rising bills.

2. Nationalisation

Bring the companies into public ownership.

How it would work
The state buys out shareholders and runs water as a public service, like Scottish Water.
For
No dividends or shareholder finance costs. Scottish Water’s finance costs are cited at about 8% of revenue, versus about 35% in England and Wales.
Against
The buy-out cost is fiercely disputed — from about £14.5bn to over £100bn depending on the method. And the £104bn investment bill still has to be paid, by billpayers or taxpayers. Not-for-profit Dŵr Cymru still has the highest projected bill by 2030 (£645).
Effect on bills
Uncertain. It changes who owns the companies, not how much needs spending — and risks creating one bigger monopoly.

3. Tougher regulation

Keep private companies, rebuild the rules — the Cunliffe Review plan.

How it would work
Replace Ofwat with one stronger regulator, set minimum capital so companies can’t load up on debt, add a binding consumer ombudsman, compulsory smart metering and a national social tariff for households that struggle.
For
This is what the government’s own review recommended in 2025 — 88 recommendations — and it tackles the failures (debt, weak oversight, sewage spills) without a buy-out.
Against
Regulation is what failed before. Cunliffe also did not consider nationalisation — it was ruled out of its brief — so critics say the biggest question was never asked.
Effect on bills
Bills still rise to pay for investment, but waste, debt costs and unfairness should fall.

Simple conclusions

  • The pipes are a natural monopoly. Duplicating water and sewer networks street by street makes no sense, so some form of monopoly — private, public or regulated — always remains.
  • Competition helps, but only around the edges. It works for new homes, big projects and billing — worth having, but on Ofwat’s own numbers it will not slash household bills.
  • Nationalisation is not a shortcut to cheap water. The upfront cost is huge and disputed, the investment bill does not shrink, and one national company would be an even bigger monopoly unless it stays split into separately compared regional companies.
  • Most specialists land in the same place: tougher regulation, competition wherever it can genuinely work, and proper help for households that cannot afford the rises. Whoever owns the companies, the investment has to be paid for — the real argument is about waste, debt and fairness, not about making that bill disappear.

Methodology & sources

The boundaries are Ofwat's official GIS shapefiles of undertaker areas, version 1_5 (April 2024), digitised by Ofwat from the legal appointment records with Ordnance Survey support and published under the Open Government Licence via the House of Commons Library. Two separate files — water-supply areas and sewerage areas — are what make the two maps possible. Scotland and Northern Ireland need no company dataset (one provider each); their outlines are ONS country boundaries (December 2024, generalised). The dotted lines drawn over both maps are the ceremonial counties — the areas defined under the Lieutenancies Act 1997: England's ceremonial counties, Scotland's lieutenancy areas, Wales's preserved counties and Northern Ireland's six counties, sourced from Ordnance Survey Boundary-Line (Northern Ireland: OSNI open data) — orientation only; they play no part in deciding who supplies the water.

Caveats, as stated by Ofwat and the Commons Library: the shapefiles are a digital representation for geospatial analysis only — the definitive legal record remains the maps in each company's instrument of appointment and later variations. Small “new appointment” (NAV) areas for individual sites and developments are excluded from this map: Ofwat lists Albion Water, Independent Water Networks, SSE Water, Leep Water Networks and Veolia Water Projects as too small to show. New inset appointments are granted every month, so very new developments may differ. One open question: Ofwat's contact page still lists Cholderton & District Water (Wiltshire), while a secondary source says it was deregulated in 2018 and absorbed into Wessex Water's area — it is not shown separately here.
