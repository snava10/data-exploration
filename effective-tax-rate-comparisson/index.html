What share of a salary actually goes in tax?

One number per place: personal income tax plus mandatory employee payroll contributions, divided by gross salary. Single employee, no children. The same payslip number in each local currency — shown at three levels, 50,000, 100,000 and 150,000 — so this compares tax systems, not living standards.

At a 50,000 salary

Starting at a gross salary of 50,000 in local currency (¥5,000,000 for Japan; NT$500,000, HK$500,000, NOK 500,000 and DKK 500,000 for Taiwan, Hong Kong, Norway and Denmark). Allowances and low starting brackets do a lot of the work at this level, so rates start lower here than at the higher salaries below. Taiwan (4.8%) is the lowest, followed by Hong Kong (11.9%).

Watch Singapore at this level. At S$50,000 income tax is only 2.5%, yet the combined rate is 22.5% — because the employee CPF contribution is the full 20% of salary, with no ceiling in the way. The CPF share is compulsory savings in the employee’s own account, not general tax revenue — so on a tax-only view Singapore would sit near the bottom of this table instead.

At a 100,000 salary

The same method at a gross salary of 100,000 in local currency (¥10,000,000 for Japan; NT$1,000,000, HK$1,000,000, NOK 1,000,000 and DKK 1,000,000 for Taiwan, Hong Kong, Norway and Denmark). Most rates rise as pay goes up from 50,000 — Singapore stands out for a different reason, as explained below — and the ranking reshuffles. Taiwan (5.6%) is still the lowest, followed by Hong Kong (14.8%).

Singapore runs the other way. At S$100,000 the CPF annual ceiling (S$102,000) still does not bind, so the employee contribution remains the full 20% of salary. The combined rate is 25.7% — up from 22.5% at S$50,000, and higher than it will be at S$150,000 (21.9% in the next section, where the ceiling finally caps the CPF share) — even though income tax alone is only 5.7%. As before, the CPF share is compulsory savings in the employee’s own account.
The UK is much lighter at £100,000. At exactly £100,000 the personal allowance is still fully intact; it is withdrawn between £100,000 and £125,140. That helps keep the UK rate at 31.4% here — it rises to 39.1% at £150,000 (below), once the allowance is fully withdrawn.

At a 150,000 salary

The same method at a gross salary of 150,000 in local currency (¥15,000,000 for Japan; NT$1,500,000, HK$1,500,000, NOK 1,500,000 and DKK 1,500,000 for Taiwan, Hong Kong, Norway and Denmark). At this level most rates are at their highest in this comparison — allowances have less effect and top brackets and contribution ceilings start to bite.

Read Singapore with care. Its 21.9% is low, but only 8.3 points are income tax. The other 13.6 points are employee CPF contributions (20% rate, capped at the S$102,000 annual ceiling) — compulsory savings in the individual’s own account, not general tax revenue. Estonia’s figure similarly includes a 2% funded-pension contribution to the employee’s own pension account. Taiwan (NT$1,500,000, 8.1%) remains the lowest in this table too, followed by Hong Kong (HK$1,500,000, 15.5%).

How to read this

One number (%) — that number counts mandatory employee payroll contributions (state pension, health, unemployment insurance) as tax. That is the standard payslip view: it is money you do not receive. Where part of it is really your own savings — Singapore CPF, Hong Kong’s MPF, Estonia’s funded pension — the table flags it.

What is left out: VAT / sales tax and other consumption taxes, property taxes, and employer-side contributions. Employer contributions are large in several European systems. For context, OECD Taxing Wages 2026 puts the full tax wedge including employer costs at the average wage at 52.5% in Belgium, 49.3% in Germany and 47.2% in France — a different, broader measure than the employee-payslip rate used here.

Same number, different lives. 50,000, 100,000 or 150,000 buys very different living standards in Zurich, Madrid and Singapore. This page compares how each system treats the same payslip figure, not purchasing power.

Place matters inside countries too: US states, Swiss cantons, Canadian provinces (Ontario used here), Spanish autonomous communities (Madrid used here) and Italian regional surtaxes all move the number. New York City adds roughly 3.5 points on top of New York State.

Method in brief

For each jurisdiction: apply the official 2026 (or latest 2025) income-tax schedule, standard deductions/allowances and credits available to a single childless employee, plus mandatory employee social contributions with their official caps and ceilings, to gross salaries of 50,000, 100,000 and 150,000 in local currency. The scaled-currency rows — Japan (¥5,000,000 / ¥10,000,000 / ¥15,000,000), Taiwan (NT$500,000 / NT$1,000,000 / NT$1,500,000), Hong Kong (HK$500,000 / HK$1,000,000 / HK$1,500,000), Norway (NOK 500,000 / NOK 1,000,000 / NOK 1,500,000) and Denmark (DKK 500,000 / DKK 1,000,000 / DKK 1,500,000) — represent a comparable professional salary level in local currency, because 50,000 / 100,000 / 150,000 in those currencies is not a viable professional salary (NT$150,000/year is below Taiwan’s minimum wage). Canada uses CAD 50,000 / CAD 100,000 / CAD 150,000 at face value. Divide the total by gross salary. Figures cross-checked for ordering against OECD Taxing Wages 2026 (2025 data).

  • UK at £150,000: personal allowance fully withdrawn; income tax 35.8% + employee National Insurance 3.3%.
  • Germany excludes church tax; employee social contributions are capped, hence 11.7% of gross at this level.
  • Netherlands: labour and general tax credits are fully phased out at this income; occupational pension contributions (employer-specific) excluded.
  • Switzerland (Zurich) excludes mandatory private health-insurance premiums, roughly 3% of salary.
  • Australia includes the 2% Medicare levy.
  • Italy (Rome): IRPEF (23% / 33% / 43% for 2026) plus the Lazio regional surtax (3.33% at this income) and the Rome municipal surtax (0.9%); employee INPS contributions (9.19% IVS plus the 1% additional contribution above the first pension band) are subject to the official annual ceiling.
  • UK at £100,000: personal allowance fully intact (it is withdrawn between £100,000 and £125,140) — hence 31.4% here, rising to 39.1% at £150,000 once the allowance is gone.
  • Singapore at S$100,000: the S$102,000 CPF ceiling still does not bind, so the full 20% employee CPF contribution applies — the combined rate (25.7%) is higher than at S$150,000 (21.9%), where the ceiling caps the contribution, even though income tax is lower at S$100,000.
  • Germany at €100,000: employee contribution ceilings are high relative to salary, so contributions stay a large share of pay (41.5% overall).
  • Taiwan (NT$1,500,000): income tax only ~5.7%; employee labour + health insurance ~2.4% — insured-salary caps keep contributions low. At NT$1,000,000 the combined rate is 5.6%, and at NT$500,000 it is 4.8% — Taiwan is the lowest at all three salary levels shown.
  • Hong Kong: salaries tax plus the Mandatory Provident Fund (MPF) employee contribution — 5% capped at HK$18,000/year (1.2% of HK$1,500,000; 1.8% of HK$1,000,000). MPF is the individual’s own retirement account, like Singapore’s CPF.
  • Canada (Ontario): federal + Ontario income tax plus Canada Pension Plan (including CPP2) and Employment Insurance. Ontario is the most populous province; other provinces differ.
  • Norway: 22% ordinary income tax + bracket tax (trinnskatt) + 7.8% employee national insurance — 22.2% at NOK 500,000, 31.4% at NOK 1,000,000 (level with the UK at that salary), rising to 36.5% at NOK 1,500,000.
  • Denmark: 8% AM-bidrag (labour-market contribution) + bottom-bracket, top-bracket and average municipal taxes — 34.2% at DKK 500,000, 44.8% at DKK 1,000,000 (second-highest at that level) and 48.5% at DKK 1,500,000 (just below Italy). Denmark’s figures use 2025 rules; a Danish tax reform takes effect from 2026.
